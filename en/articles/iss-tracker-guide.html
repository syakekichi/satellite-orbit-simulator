1. What is the International Space Station (ISS)?

The International Space Station (ISS) is the largest habitable artificial satellite in low Earth orbit (LEO), orbiting at an altitude of approximately 400 kilometers (250 miles). It represents a joint multinational collaborative project between NASA (USA), Roscosmos (Russia), JAXA (Japan), ESA (Europe), and CSA (Canada). Spanning 108 meters by 73 meters—about the size of a football field—it has maintained continuous human presence since November 2000.

2. Why is it Visible to the Naked Eye?

The ISS does not produce its own external light. Instead, it shines brightly because its massive solar array panels, covering an area of over 2,400 square meters, reflect sunlight back to Earth. During twilight hours just after dusk or before dawn, the ground is dark while the station 400 km above is bathed in bright sunlight, making it appear brighter than Venus in the night sky.

💡 How to Distinguish from Airplanes

The ISS moves smoothly across the sky without flashing strobe lights or making any engine noise. It looks like a bright, constant white star traveling swiftly over 3 to 5 minutes.

3. Orbital Mechanics: 400 km Altitude & 27,700 km/h Speed

To stay in orbit without falling to Earth, the ISS travels at an astonishing speed of 7.66 km/s (approx. 27,700 km/h or 17,200 mph). It completes a full orbit around Earth every 92.6 minutes, meaning astronauts experience 16 sunrises and sunsets every single day.

4. 3 Steps to Spot ISS Overhead

  1. Check Pass Times: Use SatViewer3D's pass prediction tool to get exact local rise, peak, and set times.
  2. Look for High Elevations: Passes reaching 45 degrees or higher above the horizon offer the clearest, brightest views.
  3. Look Up 2 Minutes Early: Step outside away from bright streetlights and watch the station glide silently across the constellations.

5. Scientific Significance of the Japanese 'Kibo' Laboratory

JAXA's Japanese Experiment Module (JEM), nicknamed 'Kibo' (Hope), is the largest single module on the ISS. Equipped with its own robotic arm, scientific airlock, and external exposed facility, it leads global experiments in microgravity protein crystallization, new material sciences, and CubeSat deployments.
